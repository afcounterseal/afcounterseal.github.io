Questions investors ask.

"A large wallet company can copy this."See the comparison below: the obstacle is their own product, not their ability. The arrangement is also the subject of pending patent applications and two granted patents.
"Building on the Internet Computer is a narrow bet. What if the platform fails?"The design includes a standby that takes over under rules fixed in advance, with no company in the path. The trade we accept, stated openly: the machines of the Internet Computer are admitted by that network's governance, not by open participation. In return, every machine must run the same published software, which is the property a wallet needs most.
"Is a blockchain really safer than a well-run company's servers?"The two fail differently. A company's servers can be taken over through one administrator, one stolen credential or one altered file; that is how the Bybit and Binance thefts began, and in the first quarter of 2025 such services accounted for 88% of everything stolen. On a blockchain an attacker needs many independent parties to act together, and every change to the code is public before it takes effect. What a Counterseal subscriber relies on, stated in full: the machines of the Internet Computer are run by known, independent providers, and signing needs more than one third of them to act together; changes to the platform are public proposals; changes to the wallet are public votes by token holders, with a waiting period in which a subscriber can withdraw. No company, operator or single person has to be trusted.
"Privacy is too heavy for a hardware wallet."It is, when every payment must be published with a proof. Payments inside Counterseal are not published, so the first privacy level needs no proof on the device, and it works for assets from chains that cannot run a privacy contract, Bitcoin included.
"Small payments have never worked. Why now?"People will not decide on a purchase of a fraction of a cent; software agents will. An agent also needs spending limits its owner controls. Counterseal's device shows the owner what an agent tries to spend and enforces limits on amount, frequency and recipient. Payment rails are planned for a later release.
"Who gets paid, and how does Counterseal, Inc. earn?"The two are separate. The service is paid for by fees that its governance sets and that go to the protocol's own treasury, which also pays the service's running costs. Counterseal, Inc. receives none of those fees. Its income is the device it sells, as one manufacturer among possibly several, and licences for its software and patents. Fee levels and the terms of an investment in Counterseal, Inc. are not published here; they are covered in the investor presentation.
"What stops stolen funds from entering?"Funds entering the Ledger must come with a proof that they do not originate from addresses on public lists of thefts and sanctioned sources. The proof is produced on the owner's side and reveals nothing else about the owner's history. No company examines or identifies depositors.
"You depend on others for trade execution. Where is your advantage?"Execution can be bought from many parties. The advantage is what reaches us before execution: the flow. Every subscriber's transaction converges on the same signer, which runs on a blockchain, and it arrives there unsigned.

Other wallets sign first. Each transaction is signed on the user's phone or hardware wallet before it leaves, so whoever collects it afterwards receives a finished transaction that can only be routed and put in order. MetaMask, for example, sends eligible transactions to a private auction in which block builders (the parties that assemble blocks) bid to include them. That protects users from being traded against, but every transaction in that auction is already signed, so none can be changed, matched against another user's or merged with it.

Counterseal signs last. Each owner confirms limits, not a finished transaction, so when the flow has gathered, the form of every transaction in it is still open. Counterseal can do what a sign-first wallet does with its flow: deliver it privately and let block builders compete for it. It can also do what cannot be done to a transaction that is already signed, which is to change it. Every result is checked against what its owner approved before anything is signed. That is weaving.

Weaving is much more than matching buyers with sellers. Matching is one use. Counterseal can also combine many transactions into one, keep steps that depend on each other in one unit that succeeds or fails as a whole, send a trade to a different exchange or swap service from the one it was written for, where its owner allows that, and hand the finished flow to whichever block builder offers the best terms. Only matching needs a counterparty among subscribers. The others work on any flow, and all of them are worth more as the flow grows: more matches, larger combined transactions, and better terms from block builders. The savings go back to subscribers. Why the incumbents cannot simply copy it

Every building block is public. What does not exist elsewhere is the arrangement. A hardware-wallet maker would have to stop being the display that the phone writes to. A phone or browser wallet would have to move signing off the phone. Each would be rebuilding against its own product and its own installed base.

Release plan.

Release 1.0The wallet: the app, the Counterseal Token, confirmation of every transaction on the device, and payments that are private from the public.
Release 2.0Payment rails for commerce and for software agents, with the owner's limits enforced and shown on the device.
Release 3.0The strictest privacy: the Ledger's code no longer sees amounts, and each payment carries a mathematical proof prepared in advance.

The plan describes intentions and may change. No dates are given.
