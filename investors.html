Questions investors ask.

"A large wallet company can copy this."See the comparison below: the obstacle is their own product, not their ability. The arrangement is also the subject of pending patent applications and two granted patents.
"Building on the Internet Computer is a narrow bet. What if the platform fails?"Judge a blockchain by its own failures, not by the bugs of the applications built on it. Nobody counts a faulty Ethereum application against Ethereum. Since the Internet Computer started in 2021, no reported theft has come from the platform itself: not from the agreement among its machines, not from its signing cryptography, and not from one smart contract reaching into another — a smart contract being a program that lives on the blockchain itself — which is the separation every application on it depends on. The thefts that have been reported all came from flaws in individual applications. The platform's own reported failures have been temporary stoppages or slowdowns, each recovered with no reported loss of data. A phone or PC cannot say the same: in 2021 the Pegasus spyware took over fully updated iPhones without the owner doing anything, by getting past the protection Apple had added to stop that kind of attack. On a phone, a wallet with no bugs of its own can still be emptied. For a failure that is not recovered, the design includes a standby that takes over under rules fixed in advance, with no company in the path. The trade we accept, stated openly: the machines of the Internet Computer are admitted by that network's governance, not by open participation. In return, every machine must run the same published software, which is the property a wallet needs most.
"What is the remaining risk on the Internet Computer?"One compromised machine changes no result and produces no signature. The signing key is held, in shares, by a group of 34 machines, each run by a different operator, in 24 countries (the map), and a signature needs more than one third of them — 12 — to act together. If that many did, they could sign without the wallet's code, and so without the owner's confirmation. What one compromised machine can do is read the data of the smart contracts it runs. That is why Counterseal is designed to keep your private records where those machines cannot read them: at the Shielded level they are stored in a form readable only inside your own Counterseal Token hardware device.
"Is a blockchain really safer than a well-run company's servers?"The two fail differently. A company's servers can be taken over through one administrator, one stolen credential or one altered file; that is how the Bybit and Binance thefts began, and in the first quarter of 2025 such services accounted for 88% of everything stolen. On a blockchain an attacker needs many independent parties to act together, and every change to the code is public before it takes effect. What a Counterseal subscriber relies on, stated in full: the machines of the Internet Computer are run by known, independent providers, and signing needs more than one third of them to act together; changes to the platform are public proposals; changes to the wallet are public votes by token holders, with a waiting period in which a subscriber can withdraw. No company, operator or single person has to be trusted.
"Zengo and Fireblocks already split the signing key. Isn't that the same thing?"Splitting a key answers one question: can one party take your funds alone. It does not answer the other: what decides when a signature is produced, and who has to keep acting for you to use your own money. Binance's wallet system used split-key signing, in which several parties each hold a share and sign together so that no one holds the whole key, and the largest theft on record still happened, because the attacker reached the system that decides what gets signed. Binance's own report says that because the wallet used split keys there was no single key with which to sign a cancellation, and a special procedure had to be developed while the attack was running. Part 4 → The second question is also where these services differ from Counterseal. A company-run signing service holds a share and takes part in every transaction, so you cannot sign while it is down, and you depend on it continuing to cooperate. Each publishes a plan for its own failure. Zengo's, by its own description, places a single global decryption key with an escrow company and appoints a law office to watch for proof that Zengo is still operating; if it is not, the key is published on a public repository, and each customer's app uses it to unlock the company's share. Fireblocks asks the customer to keep its own backup shares; in 2021 a customer sued it in Tel Aviv over key shares that were lost, each side saying the other was responsible for the backup. In Counterseal no company holds a share and none takes part. The shares are held by machines of the Internet Computer, run by independent operators. If Counterseal, Inc. stopped existing tomorrow, nothing would have to be released, published or decided by anyone: the code keeps running and keeps obeying confirmations from your device. Statements here about other companies come from their own published documentation and from court reporting, checked in November 2026.
"Institutions approve transactions through services like Safe. Where does Counterseal fit?"Those services hold no key and have no authority over the account, which is the right design as far as it goes. What they do is store a proposed transaction and collect each owner's signature. Every owner reads the proposal in an ordinary browser application, and the hardware wallet that signs it often shows only an unreadable string of characters. The meaning of the transaction is therefore settled in the browser. That is the path the Bybit attackers took: the signers were shown a routine transfer, their devices showed raw data, and what they signed was a transaction that handed over the wallet. Part 1 → The same account, held in Counterseal, is arranged differently. The meaning of each transaction is worked out on a blockchain from the transaction itself, not in anyone's browser, and is shown on each approver's own device. The account's rules — which people must approve, how many of them, what limits and destinations are allowed — are applied by the same code that produces the signature. There is no contract to deploy, no hosted web page in the path, and no key on any approver's device. Stated plainly: we are building the wallet for individuals first, and this is not in the release plan above. An institution would be a subscriber like any other, Counterseal, Inc. would operate nothing, and the protection still rests on code that must be correct and that changes only by a public governance vote.
"Privacy is too heavy for a hardware wallet."It is, when every payment must be published with a zero-knowledge proof (a zero-knowledge proof: a short piece of data that lets anyone check that a statement is true without learning anything else). Payments inside Counterseal are not published, so the first privacy level needs no such proof on the device, and it works for assets from chains that cannot run a privacy contract, Bitcoin included.
"Small payments have never worked. Why now?"People will not decide on a purchase of a fraction of a cent; software agents will. An agent also needs spending limits its owner controls. Counterseal's device shows the owner what an agent tries to spend and enforces limits on amount, frequency and recipient. Payment rails are planned for a later release.
"Who gets paid, and how does Counterseal, Inc. earn?"The two are separate. The service is paid for by fees that its governance sets and that go to the protocol's own treasury, which also pays the service's running costs. Counterseal, Inc. receives none of those fees. Its income is the device it sells, as one manufacturer among possibly several, and licences for its software and patents. Fee levels and the terms of an investment in Counterseal, Inc. are not published here; they are covered in the investor presentation.
"What stops stolen funds from entering?"Funds entering the private ledger must come with a zero-knowledge proof that they do not originate from addresses on public lists of thefts and sanctioned sources. The proof is produced on the owner's side and reveals nothing else about the owner's history. No company examines or identifies depositors.
"You depend on others for trade execution. Where is your advantage?"Execution can be bought from many parties. The advantage is what reaches us before execution: the flow. Every subscriber's transaction converges on the same signer, which runs on a blockchain, and it arrives there unsigned.

Other wallets sign first. Each transaction is signed on the user's phone or hardware wallet before it leaves, so whoever collects it afterwards receives a finished transaction that can only be routed and put in order. MetaMask, for example, sends eligible transactions to a private auction in which block builders (the parties that assemble blocks) bid to include them. That protects users from being traded against, but every transaction in that auction is already signed, so none can be changed, matched against another user's or merged with it.

Counterseal signs last. Each owner confirms limits, not a finished transaction, so when the flow has gathered, the form of every transaction in it is still open. Counterseal can do what a sign-first wallet does with its flow: deliver it privately and let block builders compete for it. It can also do what cannot be done to a transaction that is already signed, which is to change it. Every result is checked against what its owner approved before anything is signed. That is weaving.

Weaving is much more than matching buyers with sellers. Matching is one use. Counterseal can also combine many transactions into one, keep steps that depend on each other in one unit that succeeds or fails as a whole, send a trade to a different exchange or swap service from the one it was written for, where its owner allows that, and hand the finished flow to whichever block builder offers the best terms. Only matching needs a counterparty among subscribers. The others work on any flow, and all of them are worth more as the flow grows: more matches, larger combined transactions, and better terms from block builders. The savings go back to subscribers. Where the machines are.

The key that signs your transactions is never assembled anywhere. It exists only as shares held by the machines of one group inside the Internet Computer, and a signature is produced only when enough of them take part. How many machines a smart contract runs on is chosen when it is published: the ordinary group is thirteen, and Counterseal takes the largest the network offers for the part that deals with the signing keys.

34 machines, 34 operators

In 24 countries, as the Internet Computer's own dashboard showed on 17 November 2026. No two of them sit in the same data centre, and no two are run by the same operator. No more than three are in any one country.

12 would have to act together

More than one third of the group. Fewer than that change no result and produce no signature. None of them is Counterseal, Inc.

A second group of 34

It holds a backup copy of the shares, in the same divided form, and answers no signing request.

A company-run signing service divides the same job between two parties, and one of them is the company. That is the comparison to hold in mind: not how the key is split, but how many independent parties would have to act together, and whether any of them is a company that can fail, be bought or be ordered to stop. Why the incumbents cannot simply copy it

Every building block is public. What does not exist elsewhere is the arrangement. A hardware-wallet maker would have to stop being the display that the phone writes to. A phone or browser wallet would have to move signing off the phone. Each would be rebuilding against its own product and its own installed base.

Release plan.

Release 1.0The wallet: the app, the Counterseal Token, confirmation of every transaction on the device, and payments that are private from the public.
Release 2.0Payment rails for commerce and for software agents, with the owner's limits enforced and shown on the device.
Release 3.0The Shielded level: balances and history kept by the private ledger in a form only your own device can read. Then the strictest level: the ledger's code no longer sees amounts, and each payment carries a zero-knowledge proof prepared in advance.

The plan describes intentions and may change. No dates are given.
